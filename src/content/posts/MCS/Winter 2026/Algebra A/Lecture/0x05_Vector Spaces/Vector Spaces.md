- **Example 1**
  1. Consider
     $$
     \mathbb R^2=\{(a,b):a,b\in\mathbb R\}.
     $$
     We have
     $$
     (a,b)+(c,d)=(a+c,b+d)
     $$
     and
     $$
     \lambda(a,b)=(\lambda a,\lambda b).
     $$

  2. Consider
     $$
     \mathbb R^n
     =\{(a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n):a_i\in\mathbb R\text{ for every }i\}.
     $$
     We have
     $$
     (a_1,\ldots,a_n)+(b_1,\ldots,b_n)
     =(a_1+b_1,\ldots,a_n+b_n)
     $$
     and
     $$
     \lambda(a_1,\ldots,a_n)
     =(\lambda a_1,\ldots,\lambda a_n).
     $$

  3. In $\mathbb R[x]$, we have polynomial addition $p(x)+q(x)$ and scalar multiplication $\lambda p(x)$.

  4. In $M_{2\times2}(\mathbb R)$, we have matrix addition $A+B$ and scalar multiplication $\lambda A$.

- **Definition 2**
  A vector space over $\mathbb F$ is a set $V$ equipped with the following operations.

  1. An addition
     $$
     +\colon V\times V\longrightarrow V
     $$
     satisfying, for all $u,v,w\in V$,
     $$
     (u+v)+w=u+(v+w),
     \qquad
     u+v=v+u,
     $$
     together with the existence of an element $0\in V$ such that
     $$
     v+0=v,
     $$
     and, for every $v\in V$, the existence of an element $-v\in V$ such that
     $$
     v+(-v)=0.
     $$

  2. A scalar multiplication
     $$
     \cdot\colon\mathbb F\times V\longrightarrow V
     $$
     satisfying, for all $v,w\in V$ and $\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb F$,
     $$
     1\cdot v=v,
     \qquad
     \mu(\lambda v)=(\mu\lambda)v,
     $$
     $$
     \lambda(v+w)=\lambda v+\lambda w,
     \qquad
     (\lambda+\mu)v=\lambda v+\mu v.
     $$

  - **Notation 2.1**
    The elements of $V$ are called vectors.

  - **Observation 2.2**
    The zero vector $0$ is unique, and the additive inverse $-v$ of each $v\in V$ is unique. Moreover,
    $$
    0\cdot v=0
    \qquad\text{and}\qquad
    (-1)v=-v.
    $$

    - **Proof 2.2.1**
      Suppose that $0$ and $0'$ are both zero vectors. Then
      $$
      0=0+0'=0',
      $$
      so the zero vector is unique.

      Suppose that $w$ and $w'$ are both additive inverses of $v$. Then
      $$
      w=w+0=w+(v+w')=(w+v)+w'=0+w'=w',
      $$
      so the additive inverse of $v$ is unique.

      By distributivity,
      $$
      0\cdot v=(0+0)v=0\cdot v+0\cdot v.
      $$
      Adding the inverse of $0\cdot v$ to both sides gives $0\cdot v=0$.

      Finally,
      $$
      v+(-1)v=1v+(-1)v=(1+(-1))v=0\cdot v=0.
      $$
      Hence $(-1)v$ is the additive inverse of $v$, and therefore $(-1)v=-v$.

  - **Example 2.3**
    1. Let
       $$
       V=\mathbb F^n
       =\{(a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n):a_i\in\mathbb F\}.
       $$
       Then
       $$
       (a_1,\ldots,a_n)+(b_1,\ldots,b_n)
       =(a_1+b_1,\ldots,a_n+b_n)
       $$
       and
       $$
       \lambda(a_1,\ldots,a_n)
       =(\lambda a_1,\ldots,\lambda a_n).
       $$
       Check that these operations satisfy the eight defining properties.

    2. Let $V=M_{m\times n}(\mathbb F)$. The operations $A+B$ and $\lambda A$ are defined entrywise.

    3. Let $V=\mathbb F[x]$. The operations $p(x)+q(x)$ and $\lambda p(x)$ are defined coefficientwise.

    4. Let
       $$
       V=\mathbb F_n[x]
       =\{p(x)\in\mathbb F[x]:\deg p(x)\leqslant n\}.
       $$
       The operations $p(x)+q(x)$ and $\lambda p(x)$ are defined coefficientwise.

    5. Let
       $$
       V=\mathcal F(X,\mathbb F)
       =\{f\colon X\to\mathbb F\}.
       $$
       We have
       $$
       (f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x)
       \qquad\text{and}\qquad
       (\lambda f)(x)=\lambda f(x).
       $$

    6. Let $\mathbb F=\mathbb R$ and $V=\mathbb C$. Addition is the usual addition in $\mathbb C$. Is this vector space $\mathbb C^1$?

    - **Answer 2.3.1**
      No, not with the standard choice of base field. Here, $V=\mathbb C$ is regarded as a vector space over $\mathbb R$, whereas $\mathbb C^1$ is naturally a vector space over $\mathbb C$.

      As a real vector space, $V$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb R^2$ via
      $$
      \Phi\colon\mathbb C\longrightarrow\mathbb R^2,
      \qquad
      \Phi(a+bi)=(a,b).
      $$
      Indeed, for all $z,w\in\mathbb C$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb R$,
      $$
      \Phi(z+w)=\Phi(z)+\Phi(w)
      \qquad\text{and}\qquad
      \Phi(\lambda z)=\lambda\Phi(z).
      $$

  - **Exercise 2.4**
    1. Let $V=\mathbb C^3$. The line generated by $(1,0,i)$ is
       $$
       \{(t,0,ti):t\in\mathbb C\}.
       $$

    2. Let $V=\mathbb Z_5^3$. What is the line generated by $(1,2,3)$?
       $$
       (0,0,0),(1,2,3),(2,4,1),(3,1,4),(4,3,2).
       $$

    3. We have
       $$
       V=\mathcal F(\{*,\#\},\mathbb F)
       \sim\mathbb F\times\mathbb F
       =\mathbb F^2.
       $$
       Is this bijection compatible with the vector space structure?

    - **Solution 2.4.1**
      Define
      $$
      \Phi\colon\mathcal F(\{*,\#\},\mathbb F)\longrightarrow\mathbb F^2,
      \qquad
      \Phi(f)=(f(*),f(\#)).
      $$
      This map is bijective because, for every $(a,b)\in\mathbb F^2$, there is exactly one function $f\colon\{*,\#\}\to\mathbb F$ satisfying
      $$
      f(*)=a
      \qquad\text{and}\qquad
      f(\#)=b.
      $$

      For all $f,g\in\mathcal F(\{*,\#\},\mathbb F)$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb F$, we have
      $$
      \begin{aligned}
      \Phi(f+g)
      &=((f+g)(*),(f+g)(\#))\\
      &=(f(*)+g(*),f(\#)+g(\#))\\
      &=\Phi(f)+\Phi(g)
      \end{aligned}
      $$
      and
      $$
      \begin{aligned}
      \Phi(\lambda f)
      &=((\lambda f)(*),(\lambda f)(\#))\\
      &=(\lambda f(*),\lambda f(\#))\\
      &=\lambda\Phi(f).
      \end{aligned}
      $$
      Therefore, the bijection is compatible with the vector space structure.

- **Challenge 3**
  Let $V=M_{2\times2}(\mathbb F)$. Show that there exist $M_1,M_2,M_3,M_4\in V$ such that, for every $A\in V$, there are scalars $\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3,\lambda_4\in\mathbb F$ satisfying
  $$
  A=\lambda_1M_1+\lambda_2M_2+\lambda_3M_3+\lambda_4M_4.
  $$

  - **Solution 3.1**
    Choose
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    M_1&=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},
    &
    M_2&=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\\[6pt]
    M_3&=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix},
    &
    M_4&=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    For an arbitrary matrix
    $$
    A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix},
    $$
    take
    $$
    \lambda_1=a,\qquad
    \lambda_2=b,\qquad
    \lambda_3=c,\qquad
    \lambda_4=d.
    $$
    Then
    $$
    A=aM_1+bM_2+cM_3+dM_4.
    $$

  - **Notation 3.2**
    These matrices are denoted by
    $$
    M_1=E_{11},\qquad
    M_2=E_{12},\qquad
    M_3=E_{21},\qquad
    M_4=E_{22}.
    $$

- **Remark 3.3**
  Assume that $i\in\mathbb F$. Is it possible to choose
  $$
  M_1=\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\0&0\end{pmatrix}?
  $$

  - **Solution 3.3.1**
    Yes. Choose
    $$
    M_2=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},
    \qquad
    M_3=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix},
    \qquad
    M_4=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
    $$
    If
    $$
    A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix},
    $$
    then
    $$
    A=aM_1+(b-ai)M_2+cM_3+dM_4.
    $$

- **Challenge 4**
  Considering $\mathbb C$ as an $\mathbb R$-vector space, how many vectors are necessary to generate it?

  - **Solution 4.1**
    One vector is not enough. If $v=0$, then its real scalar multiples generate only $\{0\}$. If $v\neq0$ and $\mathbb C$ were generated by $v$, then $iv$ would have to equal $\lambda v$ for some $\lambda\in\mathbb R$. Since $v\neq0$, this would imply $\lambda=i$, which is impossible because $i\notin\mathbb R$.

    Two vectors are enough. Indeed, every $z=a+bi\in\mathbb C$ can be written as
    $$
    z=a\cdot1+b\cdot i,
    \qquad a,b\in\mathbb R.
    $$
    Therefore, the two vectors $1$ and $i$ generate $\mathbb C$ over $\mathbb R$.

- **Challenge 5**
  Consider $\mathbb R$ as a $\mathbb Q$-vector space. Do there exist real numbers $\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\ldots,\alpha_k$ such that every $r\in\mathbb R$ can be written as
  $$
  r=\lambda_1\alpha_1+\cdots+\lambda_k\alpha_k
  $$
  for some $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_k\in\mathbb Q$?

  - **Solution 5.1**
    No. For any fixed finite list $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_k$, the set
    $$
    \left\{
    \lambda_1\alpha_1+\cdots+\lambda_k\alpha_k:
    \lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_k\in\mathbb Q
    \right\}
    $$
    is the image of $\mathbb Q^k$ under the map
    $$
    (\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_k)
    \longmapsto
    \lambda_1\alpha_1+\cdots+\lambda_k\alpha_k.
    $$
    Since $\mathbb Q^k$ is countable, this image is countable. However, $\mathbb R$ is uncountable. Therefore, no finite list of real numbers can generate $\mathbb R$ as a vector space over $\mathbb Q$.

- **Remark 5.2**
  If $\mathbb E$ is a subfield of $\mathbb F$, denoted by
  $$
  \mathbb E\hookrightarrow\mathbb F,
  $$
  then every $\mathbb F$-vector space $V$ is also an $\mathbb E$-vector space.

  - **Example 5.3**
    $$
    \mathbb Q
    \hookrightarrow
    \mathbb Q[\sqrt2]
    \hookrightarrow
    \mathbb R
    \hookrightarrow
    \mathbb C.
    $$

- **Non-Example 5.4**
  The following sets are not vector spaces with the usual addition and scalar multiplication.

  1. The first quadrant of $\mathbb R^2$ is not closed under multiplication by negative scalars.
  2. The set
     $$
     \{\text{polynomials of degree }3\}\cup\{0\}
     $$
     is not closed under addition. For example, $x^3$ and $-x^3+x^2$ both have degree $3$, but their sum is $x^2$.
  3. The set
     $$
     \{(a,b):a,b\in\mathbb R\text{ and }ab\geqslant0\}
     $$
     is not closed under addition. For example, $(2,1)$ and $(-1,-2)$ belong to the set, but their sum $(1,-1)$ does not.
