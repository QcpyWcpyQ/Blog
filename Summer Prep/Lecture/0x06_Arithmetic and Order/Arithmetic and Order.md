## Arithmetic and Order

- **Definition 1**
  The set of **integers** is 
  $$
  \mathbb Z=\left\{\cdots,-3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3,\cdots\right\}.
  $$
  In this course, we   will consider that $0$ is not a natural number, that is, the set of **natural numbers** is
  $$
  \mathbb N=\left\{1,2,3,\cdots\right\}.
  $$
  We endowed the set $\mathbb Z$ with two binary operations
  $$
  \begin{aligned}
  +:&\mathbb Z\times \mathbb Z\longrightarrow \mathbb Z\quad\quad \cdot:\mathbb Z\times \mathbb Z\longrightarrow \mathbb Z \\
  &(m,n)\mapsto m+n \quad(m,n)\mapsto m\cdot n 
  \end{aligned}
  $$
  The arithmetic of $\mathbb Z$ is generated by the properties that these operations satisfy.
  Notation: $m\cdot n=mn$.

  - **Properties 1.1**

    1. If $m,n,r\in\mathbb Z$ then

    $$
    (m+n)+r=m+(n+r).\quad\text{Associativity of sum}
    $$

    2. There exists a unique element $0\in\mathbb Z$ such that for all $m\in \mathbb Z$
       $$
       m+0=m=0+m.\quad\text{Existence of identity for the sum}
       $$

    3. For each $m\in \mathbb Z$ there exists a unique element $-m\in\mathbb Z$ such that
       $$
       m+(-m)=0=(-m)+m.\quad\text{Existence of inverse for the sum}
       $$

    4. If $m,n\in\mathbb Z$ then
       $$
       m+n=n+m.\quad\text{Commutativity for the sum}
       $$

    5. If $m,n,r\in\mathbb Z$ then
       $$
       (mn)r=m(nr).\quad\text{Associativity of product}
       $$

    6. There exists a unique $1\in \mathbb Z$ such that for all $m\in\mathbb Z$
       $$
       m\cdot 1=m=1\cdot m.\quad\text{Existence of identity for the product}
       $$

    7. If $m,n,a\in\mathbb Z$ with $a\neq 0$ and $am=an$ then
       $$
       m=n.\quad\text{Cancellation law}
       $$

    8. If $m,n\in \mathbb Z$ then
       $$
       mn=nm.\quad\text{Commutativity for the product}
       $$

    9. If $m,n,r\in\mathbb Z$ then
       $$
       m(n+r)=mn+mr.\quad\text{Distributivity of product}
       $$

  - **Proposition 1.2**
    $m\cdot 0=0$ for all $m\in \mathbb Z$.

    - **Proof 1.2.1**
      $$
      \begin{aligned}
      m\cdot 0&=m\cdot(0+0)\quad&\text{Property 2} \\
      &=m\cdot 0+m\cdot 0 &\text{Property 9} \\
      \end{aligned}
      $$
      hence
      $$
      \begin{aligned}
      m\cdot 0+(-m\cdot 0)&=(m\cdot 0+m\cdot 0)+(-m\cdot 0) \\
      0&=m\cdot 0+(m\cdot 0+(-m\cdot 0)) \quad &\text{Property 1, 3} \\
      0&=m\cdot 0+ 0&\text{Property 3} \\
      0&=m\cdot 0. &\text{Property 2}
      \end{aligned}
      $$

  - **Proposition 1.3**
    For all $m,n\in\mathbb Z$, if $mn=0$ then $m=0$ or $n=0$.

    - **Proof 1.3.1**
      Let $m,n\in\mathbb Z$ such that $mn=0$. If $m=0$, there is nothing to prove.
      If $m\neq 0$, then 
      $$
      mn=0=m\cdot 0.\quad\text{Proposition 1.2}
      $$
      and by $\text{Property 7}$ we have that $n=0$.

  - **Proposition 1.4**
    $-m=(-1)\cdot m$ for all $m\in\mathbb Z$.

    - **Proof 1.4.1**
      Since
      $$
      \begin{aligned}
      m+(-1)m&=m\cdot 1+m\cdot (-1)\quad&\text{Property 6, 8} \\
      &= m(1+(-1)) &\text{Property 9} \\
      &= m\cdot 0 &\text{Property 3} \\
      &= 0.&\text{Proposition 1.2}
      \end{aligned}
      $$
      therefore $(-1)m$ is an additive inverse of $m$ and by the uniqueness of the additive inverse we have $-m=(-1)m$.

  - **Proposition 1.5**
    $-(-m)=m$ for all $m\in\mathbb Z$.

    - **Proof 1.5.1**
      Since $m+(-m)=0$, the integer $m$ is the additive inverse of $-m$ and by the uniqueness of the additive inverse we have that $m=-(-m)$.

  - **Proposition 1.6 (Rules for signs)**
    For all $m,n\in\mathbb Z$,
    $$
    (-m)n=-(mn),\quad m(-n)=-(mn),\quad (-m)(-n)=mn.
    $$

    - **Proof 1.6.1**

      1. $$
         \begin{aligned}
         mn+(-m)n&=nm+n(-m) \quad&\text{Property 8} \\
         &=n(m+(-m)) &\text{Property 9} \\
         &=n\cdot 0 &\text{Property 3} \\
         &=0. &\text{Proposition 1.2}
         \end{aligned}
         $$

         Therefore $(-m)n$ is an additive inverse of $mn$ and by the uniqueness of the additive inverse we have $(-m)n=-(mn)$.

      2. $$
         \begin{aligned}
         mn+m(-n)&=m(n+(-n)) \quad&\text{Property 9} \\
         &=m\cdot 0 &\text{Property 3} \\
         &=0. &\text{Proposition 1.2}
         \end{aligned}
         $$

         Therefore $m(-n)$ is an additive inverse of $mn$ and by the uniqueness of the additive inverse we have $m(-n)=-(mn)$.

      3. $$
         \begin{aligned}
         (-m)(-n)&=-(m(-n)) \quad&\text{Proposition 1.6(1)} \\
         &= -(-mn) &\text{Proposition 1.6(2)} \\
         &= mn. &\text{Proposition 1.5}
         \end{aligned}
         $$

- **Remark 1**
  If $m^2=n^2$ it does not imply that $m=n$, for example, $2^2=4=(-2)^2$ but $2\neq -2$. In general
  $$
  0=m^2-n^2=(m-n)(m+n).
  $$
  And by the previous proposition we have that $m-n=0\lor m+n=0$, that is, $m=n\lor m=-n$.

- **Definition 2**
  The **order** in $\mathbb Z$ is defined in the following form
  $$
  m\leqslant n\iff n=m+k\text{ for some } k\in\mathbb N_0.
  $$
  Equivalently, 
  $$
  m\leqslant n\iff n-m\in\mathbb N_0.
  $$
  The **strict order** in $\mathbb Z$ is defined in the following form
  $$
  m<n\iff m\leqslant n\land m\neq n.
  $$
  The relation \(\leqslant\) is a **total order** in $\mathbb Z$, that is, reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive and any two integers can be compared.

  - **Properties 2.1**
    1. For all $m,n\in\mathbb Z$ either $m\leqslant n$ or $n\leqslant m$.
    2. If $m,n,r\in\mathbb Z$ with $m\leqslant n$ then $m+r\leqslant n+r$.
    3. If $m,n,r\in\mathbb Z$ with $m\leqslant n\land r\geqslant 0$ then $mr\leqslant nr$.

- **Definition 3**
  The **absolute value** of an integer $m$ is
  $$
  \vert m\vert=\begin{cases}
  m\quad&(m\geqslant 0) \\
  -m&(m<0)
  \end{cases}.
  $$

  - **Proposition 3.1**
    Let $m,n\in\mathbb Z$, 

    1. $m\leqslant n$ if and only if $-n\leqslant -m$.
    2. $m^2\geqslant 0$.
    3. $m^2=0$ if and only if $m=0$.

    - **Proof 3.1.1**

      1. ($\Longrightarrow$) Suppose that $m\leqslant n$ then by $\text{Property 2}$
         $$
         \begin{aligned}
         m-(m+n)&\leqslant n-(m+n) \\
         -n &\leqslant -m.
         \end{aligned}
         $$
         ($\Longleftarrow$) Suppose that $-n\leqslant -m$, then by $\text{Property 2}$
         $$
         \begin{aligned}
         -n+(n+m)&\leqslant -m+(n+m) \\
         m &\leqslant n.
         \end{aligned}
         $$

      2. If $m\geqslant 0$ then by $\text{Property 3}$
         $$
         m^2=m\cdot m\geqslant m\cdot 0=0.
         $$
         If $m\leqslant 0$ then by $\text{Proposition 3.1(1)}$ $-m\geqslant 0$ and by $\text{Property 3}$ we have
         $$
         m^2=(-m)(-m)\geqslant (-m)\cdot 0=0.
         $$

      3. ($\Longrightarrow$) If $m^2=0$ then $m\cdot m=m^2=0$ and thus $m=0$.
         ($\Longleftarrow$) If $m=0$ then $m^2=m\cdot m=0\cdot 0=0$.

- **Definition 4**
  A integer $n$ is **even** if $n=2k$ for some $k\in\mathbb Z$.
  A integer $n$ is **odd** if $n=2k+1$ for some $k\in\mathbb Z$.

  - **Example 4.1**
    $-10$ is even since $-10=2\cdot (-5)$.
    $7$ is even since $7=2\cdot 3+1$.

  - **Example 4.2**
    Prove that the sum and product of two even integers is even.

    - **Proof 4.2.1**
      Suppose that $n,n^\prime$ are even. Then by the definition
      $$
      n=2k,n^\prime=2k^\prime \text{ for some }k,k^\prime \in\mathbb Z
      $$
      hence
      $$
      n+n^\prime=2k+2k^\prime=2\left(k+k^\prime\right)\text{ with }k+k^\prime\in\mathbb Z \\
      n\cdot n^\prime = (2k)\cdot \left(2k^\prime\right) = 2 \cdot \left(2kk^\prime\right) \text{ with } 2kk^\prime\in\mathbb Z
      $$
      Thus that the sum and product of two even integers is even.
    
  - **Exercise 4.3**
    Are the sum and product of two odd integers odd?
  
    - **Solution 4.3.1**
      Suppose that $n,n^\prime$ are odd. Then by the definition
      $$
      n=2k+1,n^\prime=2k^\prime+1 \text{ for some }k,k^\prime \in\mathbb Z
      $$
      hence
      $$
      n+n^\prime=(2k+1)+\left(2k^\prime+1\right)=2\left(k+k^\prime+1\right)\text{ with }k+k^\prime+1\in\mathbb Z \\
      n\cdot n^\prime = (2k+1)\cdot \left(2k^\prime+1\right) = 2 \cdot \left(2kk^\prime+k+k^\prime\right) +1 \text{ with } 2kk^\prime\in\mathbb Z
      $$
      Thus the product of two odd integers is odd but the sum of them is even.
